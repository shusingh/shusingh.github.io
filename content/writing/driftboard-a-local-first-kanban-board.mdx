I had a Kanban board that worked and that I did not trust. It did the job at a demo, but it was built on `react-beautiful-dnd`, which Atlassian has since deprecated and archived, and its drag felt like a library doing its best rather than an app that had made a decision. Driftboard is the rebuild: same idea, taken seriously. A local-first board with rich cards, a detail panel, a command palette, focus mode, undo, and JSON export, all living entirely in the browser. No accounts, no backend, nothing to sync.

Most of the rebuild was ordinary. Three decisions were not, and they are the ones worth writing down.

## Which drag library to trust

The first question was which drag-and-drop foundation to build on now that the old one was archived. There were three honest options: `@hello-pangea/dnd`, the maintained fork of the archived library; Atlassian's new headless `pragmatic-drag-and-drop`, which is what Trello and Jira actually run on; and `dnd-kit`, the one most new React boards reach for.

The fork was the least work and the lowest ceiling: it clones your DOM node for the drag preview and re-renders whole lists, which is exactly where a custom card and buttery cross-column motion strain. Pragmatic is the most powerful and the most plumbing, since you write collision detection and drop indicators yourself. For a solo board that wants a bespoke lifted preview without hand-rolling the physics, dnd-kit sat in the right place: its `DragOverlay` hands you full control of the dragged element, its sortable presets cover both cards and columns, and keyboard dragging comes for free.

## Why the ghost card was flickering

The old board had a bug I had learned to live with: dragging a card across a column boundary made the drag preview jump by a frame. It is a small thing that reads as cheap, and it turned out to be the whole argument for the rewrite.

The cause is structural. When the moving element is a real list item, crossing into another column re-mounts it in a different `SortableContext`, and the re-mount costs a frame. `DragOverlay` fixes this by construction: the thing under your cursor is a separate element that never re-mounts, while the list items below it quietly rearrange. Decouple the preview from the list and the flicker has nowhere to live.

## Keeping undo honest

The last decision was about state. Cross-column dragging is genuinely stateful: as you hover, the placeholder has to appear in the target column, which means something is being reordered mid-drag. The naive version writes each of those intermediate arrangements into the store, and if the store is what undo records, a single drag becomes a dozen undo steps.

So I split the difference. During a drag, the intermediate arrangement lives in a throwaway working copy that only the board renders. The real store is written exactly once, when the card lands. Every move is one clean undo entry.

That split generalized into the whole state model. A Zustand store holds the board and every mutation as a typed action, wrapped by zundo for undo/redo and mirrored to `localStorage`. A second, deliberately non-undoable store holds theme, density, focus, filters, and selection, because undoing a card move should never rewind your theme or reopen a panel. Filtering and sort follow the same principle from the other side: they are views over the board, never mutations of it, and dragging switches off while a filter is narrowing cards so the indices you see can never disagree with the indices the store commits.

## What the constraint bought

None of this needed a server, and refusing to add one is what kept the project honest. The board is a plain object that serializes to a zod-validated JSON file you can export, re-import, and move between machines. It opens instantly because there is nothing to fetch, and it works on a plane because there is nothing to reach.

The lesson I keep relearning on these small apps is that the interesting work is rarely the feature list. It was three decisions: trust a maintained library, decouple the thing you drag from the thing you reorder, and decide precisely which state is allowed into history. The rest is just columns and cards.
